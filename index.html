Moon · 1967 Robotic lander

Surveyor 3

It arrived before the astronauts. Two years later, they returned to examine its remains.

Mission fact: Landed April 20, 1967 · Returned 6,326 television pictures
Open the story

01 · A visitor reaches the Moon

Imagine arriving at a world where no human has yet walked. On April 20, 1967, NASA's Surveyor 3 spacecraft made a soft landing on the Moon. It bounced twice before settling onto the lunar surface.

Its mission was more than simply reaching the Moon. Engineers and scientists needed information about the surface before astronauts attempted to land there. Could the lunar soil support a spacecraft? What was its texture and strength? Surveyor 3 helped investigate these questions.

02 · Investigating unfamiliar ground

Surveyor 3 carried a television camera and a surface-sampling device. Its camera returned 6,326 television pictures. Its sampler dug four trenches and performed bearing and impact tests, helping scientists study the mechanical properties of the lunar soil.

These observations contributed to the knowledge needed for the Apollo lunar landings. Surveyor 3 was not an astronaut, but its work helped people prepare for a journey that would change space exploration forever.

03 · The unexpected return

In November 1969, NASA's Apollo 12 astronauts landed near Surveyor 3. They walked to the inactive spacecraft, examined it, photographed it and recovered selected components, including its television camera and parts of its soil sampler.

Think about the significance of that moment: equipment sent to the Moon more than two years earlier became an object of scientific investigation by humans on the lunar surface. The astronauts brought selected parts back to Earth for examination—not the entire spacecraft.

04 · A machine becomes evidence

The returned components offered scientists an unusual opportunity to examine how equipment had changed after spending nearly two and a half years on the lunar surface. The spacecraft's story therefore continued after its original mission had ended.

Surveyor 3 remains on the Moon. Its surviving hardware is a reminder that exploration leaves physical traces behind, and that those traces can become valuable scientific evidence.

Mission record

  • Landing date: April 20, 1967.
  • Destination: The Moon.
  • Main equipment: Television camera and surface sampler.
  • Images returned: 6,326 television pictures.
  • Later visit: Apollo 12, November 1969.
  • What remains: The lander remains on the Moon; selected components were returned to Earth.

Moon · 1964 Lunar impact probe

Ranger 7

After years of setbacks, one spacecraft had just minutes to change what humanity knew about the Moon.

Mission fact: Launched July 28, 1964 · More than 4,300 lunar images · Deliberate impact on July 31, 1964
Open the story

01 · Thirteen failures. One new beginning.

By July 1964, reaching the Moon with a successful robotic imaging mission had become a difficult challenge for the United States. Earlier lunar exploration attempts had suffered repeated failures. NASA describes Ranger 7 as the first success after 13 consecutive failures.

On July 28, 1964, Ranger 7 launched aboard an Atlas-Agena B rocket. Its objective was unusual: it would not slow down for a soft landing or return to Earth. Instead, it would photograph the lunar surface while approaching at high speed.

Its six television cameras were the heart of the mission. The photographs would help scientists investigate the terrain and identify areas that might be suitable for future Apollo landings.

02 · Seventeen minutes to reveal a world

On July 31, 1964, Ranger 7 approached the Moon. During approximately its final 17 minutes of flight, its cameras transmitted thousands of images back to Earth.

Each new image revealed the surface in greater detail. Features that had appeared smooth or indistinct from Earth became a landscape of craters, depressions and changing terrain. Scientists could study details that earlier observations had not clearly resolved.

The images were more than spectacular views. They were engineering evidence: planners needed to understand the landscape before choosing where astronauts might land.

03 · The last photograph

Ranger 7's mission was designed to end in lunar impact. The spacecraft continued transmitting images until just before reaching the surface. NASA records that the final image arrived about 2.3 seconds before impact.

That final view was captured from approximately 1,600 feet (488 metres) above the Moon. The spacecraft then struck the lunar surface as planned, in the region later known as Mare Cognitum, meaning “Sea That Has Become Known.”

Ranger 7 did not survive to become a lander. Its achievement was the scientific information it sent home before its planned destruction.

04 · From photographs to future landings

The close-up images showed scientists the complexity of the lunar terrain and helped them evaluate potential landing regions. They strengthened confidence that carefully selected areas of the lunar maria could be considered for crewed landings.

Photographs alone could not prove exactly how strong the lunar soil was. Later Surveyor spacecraft added direct evidence by landing on the Moon and investigating its surface. Surveyor 1 achieved the first successful soft landing by a U.S. spacecraft on the Moon in June 1966.

Ranger 7 was therefore an important part of a larger chain of exploration. Its cameras helped turn the Moon from a distant, poorly understood landscape into a place that engineers could examine and plan to visit.

Mission record

  • Launch: July 28, 1964.
  • Lunar impact: July 31, 1964.
  • Launch vehicle: Atlas-Agena B.
  • Instruments: Six television cameras.
  • Imaging period: Approximately the final 17 minutes of flight.
  • Final image: Transmitted about 2.3 seconds before impact.
  • Impact region: Mare Cognitum, on the Moon.
  • Mission outcome: Successful planned impact after returning thousands of images.
  • What remains: Impact remnants at the lunar site.

A note for curious investigators

NASA's Ranger 7 sources do not all report the same image count. NASA's mission overview lists 4,316 images, while NASA's history article and photo archive report 4,308. This project therefore uses “more than 4,300 images” in the main story rather than hiding the discrepancy.

Comparing trustworthy sources is part of doing science: when records differ, investigate the evidence instead of quietly pretending the disagreement does not exist.

Moon · 1971 Crewed lunar rover

Apollo 15 Lunar Roving Vehicle

When astronauts needed to explore farther from their landing site, a small electric vehicle changed how they travelled across another world.

Mission fact: Apollo 15's rover travelled approximately 27.9 kilometres during its lunar exploration. [8]
Open the story

01 · A new kind of journey

Before Apollo 15, astronauts explored the Moon mainly on foot and had to remain relatively close to their lunar module. In 1971, Apollo 15 carried a Lunar Roving Vehicle: a battery-powered rover designed to transport astronauts and scientific equipment across the lunar surface. [8]

02 · The Moon became a field laboratory

The rover allowed astronauts David Scott and James Irwin to travel farther, visit geological features, and collect lunar samples. Their journeys helped scientists study the Moon's rocks and history. Across the mission, the crew brought approximately 77 kilograms of lunar material back to Earth. [8][9]

03 · A vehicle left behind

The rover was not brought home. After the astronauts completed their exploration and prepared to leave the Moon, it remained at the Hadley–Apennine landing site. Its unfinished journey became part of the landscape it had helped scientists investigate. [8]

04 · What remains?

The rover's purpose was never to return to Earth. It helped extend human exploration, carry instruments and samples, and make more scientific work possible during a limited surface visit. Today, its location is a reminder that some space hardware stays behind because that is how the mission was designed.

Mission record

Mission: Apollo 15, July–August 1971

Landing region: Hadley–Apennine, Moon

Rover distance: Approximately 27.9 km

Returned lunar samples: Approximately 77 kg

Legacy: Expanded the distance and range of scientific exploration possible during an Apollo lunar mission.

Question to explore: If a machine helps humans discover something extraordinary but cannot return home, should we think of it as abandoned equipment—or as part of the mission's achievement?

Mars · 2018–2022 Robotic lander

InSight

Mars looked silent from the outside. InSight arrived to discover what the planet was whispering beneath its ancient surface.

Mission fact: InSight landed at Elysium Planitia on November 26, 2018, to investigate Mars's interior. [7]
Open the story

The mission

Most Mars missions look outward at the landscape: its rocks, valleys, and ancient terrain. InSight had a different question. What was happening beneath the ground? After launching on May 5, 2018, it landed on Mars and began a mission to investigate the planet's hidden interior. [7]

Its instruments were designed to turn invisible processes into evidence. SEIS listened for vibrations caused by marsquakes and other activity. The HP³ experiment was designed to investigate how heat moves through the ground. It faced difficulties penetrating the soil and did not achieve its planned depth. Even an experiment that struggles can teach engineers what future missions must do differently. [8][9]

Listening to a restless planet

A marsquake may be too small to notice from the surface, but its vibrations can carry clues about the material through which they travel. By recording seismic waves, InSight helped scientists investigate the structure of Mars's crust, mantle, and core. Instead of digging deep into the planet, researchers could study signals travelling through it. [7][8]

InSight also helped demonstrate how small spacecraft called MarCO could relay communications during the landing. These two experimental spacecraft flew past Mars rather than landing there, testing a new approach to mission communications. [9]

Why it stopped

InSight depended on sunlight to generate electricity. Over time, dust accumulated on its solar panels, reducing the power available to operate its instruments and communications systems. The lander had not simply lost interest in Mars; it was running out of the energy needed to continue its work. [7][9]

NASA declared the mission over in December 2022 after repeated attempts to contact the lander received no response. Its scientific work ended, but the measurements it had already sent to Earth continued to help researchers understand the planet. [7]

Its legacy

InSight showed that Mars has a complex interior and gave scientists important evidence about its internal layers. The lander remains on the Martian surface, no longer sending new measurements but still representing a mission that changed what we know about another world. [7][8]

Its story also leaves an engineering challenge for the future: how can spacecraft stay powered for longer in dusty environments? Better dust management, energy systems, and mission planning could help future explorers survive for longer.

Question to explore: If a spacecraft can no longer communicate with Earth, does its mission end there—or does its legacy continue through the discoveries it already made?

NASA sources: [7] InSight mission overview · [8] InSight science instruments · [9] NASA JPL: InSight

Mars · 1997 Lander + rover

Mars Pathfinder & Sojourner

A bouncing landing, a tiny rover, and a planet holding clues to its ancient past.

Mission fact: Pathfinder landed on July 4, 1997. Sojourner was designed for a short technology-demonstration mission but operated for 83 days. [10][11]
Open the story

01 · A landing unlike any other

On July 4, 1997, a spacecraft arrived at Ares Vallis on Mars. Instead of relying on landing legs alone, Pathfinder used airbags to cushion its contact with the surface. It bounced across the Martian ground before coming to rest.

This landing system demonstrated a way to deliver robotic explorers to another world using technology designed to reduce the force of impact. Pathfinder was not just arriving at Mars; it was testing a new way to get there. [10][11]

02 · The little explorer rolls out

Pathfinder carried Sojourner, a small, six-wheeled rover that became the first wheeled robotic vehicle to operate on another planet. Once deployed, it could travel beyond the lander's immediate surroundings to investigate nearby rocks and soil.

Its cameras and scientific instruments helped researchers examine Martian materials at close range. The rover also demonstrated how a mobile robot could work alongside a stationary lander to explore more of the surrounding landscape. [10][11]

03 · Clues hidden in rocks and dust

The landscape was more than a collection of stones. Pathfinder examined rocks, soil, magnetic dust, and local weather. Some rocks and rounded pebbles were consistent with an ancient environment shaped by flowing water, offering clues about Mars's wetter past.

Pathfinder also returned thousands of images and scientific measurements. These observations helped scientists investigate the landing site's geology and atmosphere, turning a robotic visit into an opportunity to study how Mars had changed over time. [10][11]

04 · A mission ends, a legacy remains

Sojourner was designed to operate for seven days, but it continued working for 83 days. Pathfinder's final data transmission arrived on September 27, 1997. Communication eventually ended, and the lander and rover remained on Mars.

The hardware did not return to Earth. Its lasting contribution was the knowledge gained from its experiments and the demonstration that relatively small robotic explorers could investigate another planet. Pathfinder helped establish technologies and experience that informed later Mars exploration. [10][11]

Mission record

  • Launch: December 4, 1996
  • Mars landing: July 4, 1997
  • Landing region: Ares Vallis, Mars
  • Rover: Sojourner
  • Planned rover operation: 7 days
  • Actual rover operation: 83 days
  • Final data transmission: September 27, 1997
Question to explore: How could a tiny rover help scientists investigate an entire planet's history—and influence missions that came after it?

Mars · 2021–2024 Technology demonstrator

Ingenuity Mars Helicopter

Mars had never heard the sound of a human-made aircraft flying above its surface. Then Ingenuity changed what was possible.

Mission fact: Ingenuity completed 72 flights on Mars after its first powered, controlled flight on April 19, 2021. Its final flight took place on January 18, 2024. [12][13]
Open the story

01 · A flight against the odds

Flying on Earth is challenging enough. On Mars, the atmosphere is extremely thin—less than one percent of Earth's surface atmospheric density. An aircraft must generate lift in air that provides very little support.

Ingenuity was built to test whether powered, controlled flight could work under these conditions. It travelled to Mars attached to the Perseverance rover, carrying the technology needed to attempt a new kind of exploration. [12]

02 · The first flight

On April 19, 2021, Ingenuity completed the first powered, controlled flight on another planet. It rose above the Martian surface, hovered briefly, and landed again.

The flight was a technology demonstration, not a search for life or a mission to collect rock samples. Its purpose was to test a new capability. Engineers had planned up to five flights over roughly 30 days, enough to establish whether this kind of aircraft could work on Mars. [12][13]

03 · Beyond the original plan

Ingenuity did far more than its original demonstration required. It completed 72 flights over almost three years, helping demonstrate how aerial vehicles could observe terrain from above and provide useful information for future exploration.

Its extended mission offered engineers experience with flight operations in the Martian environment. It also showed how a small aircraft might complement a rover by examining landscapes from a different viewpoint. [12][13]

04 · The final flight

Ingenuity's 72nd flight took place on January 18, 2024. The helicopter experienced damage to its rotor blades and could no longer fly. NASA announced the end of the mission on January 25, 2024.

Although the aircraft could no longer continue flying, the engineering data gathered throughout its mission remained valuable. The helicopter had demonstrated a new way to explore another world, and its lessons could help engineers design future aerial explorers. [13][14]

Mission record

  • Arrival at Mars: February 2021, with Perseverance
  • First powered, controlled flight: April 19, 2021
  • Original flight plan: Up to 5 flights
  • Total flights completed: 72
  • Final flight: January 18, 2024
  • Mission conclusion announced: January 25, 2024
  • Final location: Mars, near Perseverance's exploration area
Question to explore: If a small helicopter can fly in the thin atmosphere of Mars, how might future aerial explorers help us investigate places that rovers cannot easily reach?

NASA sources: [12] Ingenuity Mars Helicopter mission page [13] NASA mission-end announcement [14] Ingenuity at its final airfield

Beyond the mission

Hardware stays. Knowledge travels.

Not every abandoned machine tells the same story. Ranger 7 was designed to impact the Moon. Surveyor 3 became a place astronauts could visit. InSight lost the power needed to keep working. Ingenuity exceeded its flight plan by a remarkable margin. Their endings differ—but each left evidence, engineering lessons, or new questions for future explorers.

The central idea: “abandoned” does not mean “meaningless.” A mission’s legacy includes what it discovered, what engineers learned, and what future explorers can still study.
