Moon · 1967 Robotic lander

Surveyor 3

It arrived before the astronauts. Two years later, they returned to examine its remains.

Mission fact: Landed April 20, 1967 · Returned 6,326 television pictures
Open the story

01 · A visitor reaches the Moon

Imagine arriving at a world where no human has yet walked. On April 20, 1967, NASA's Surveyor 3 spacecraft made a soft landing on the Moon. It bounced twice before settling onto the lunar surface.

Its mission was more than simply reaching the Moon. Engineers and scientists needed information about the surface before astronauts attempted to land there. Could the lunar soil support a spacecraft? What was its texture and strength? Surveyor 3 helped investigate these questions.

02 · Investigating unfamiliar ground

Surveyor 3 carried a television camera and a surface-sampling device. Its camera returned 6,326 television pictures. Its sampler dug four trenches and performed bearing and impact tests, helping scientists study the mechanical properties of the lunar soil.

These observations contributed to the knowledge needed for the Apollo lunar landings. Surveyor 3 was not an astronaut, but its work helped people prepare for a journey that would change space exploration forever.

03 · The unexpected return

In November 1969, NASA's Apollo 12 astronauts landed near Surveyor 3. They walked to the inactive spacecraft, examined it, photographed it and recovered selected components, including its television camera and parts of its soil sampler.

Think about the significance of that moment: equipment sent to the Moon more than two years earlier became an object of scientific investigation by humans on the lunar surface. The astronauts brought selected parts back to Earth for examination—not the entire spacecraft.

04 · A machine becomes evidence

The returned components offered scientists an unusual opportunity to examine how equipment had changed after spending nearly two and a half years on the lunar surface. The spacecraft's story therefore continued after its original mission had ended.

Surveyor 3 remains on the Moon. Its surviving hardware is a reminder that exploration leaves physical traces behind, and that those traces can become valuable scientific evidence.

Mission record

  • Landing date: April 20, 1967.
  • Destination: The Moon.
  • Main equipment: Television camera and surface sampler.
  • Images returned: 6,326 television pictures.
  • Later visit: Apollo 12, November 1969.
  • What remains: The lander remains on the Moon; selected components were returned to Earth.

Moon · 1964Impact probe

Ranger 7

A spacecraft designed to end its journey by crashing—while its cameras kept working until the final seconds.

Mission fact: Returned 4,316 images before lunar impact.
Open the story

The mission

Ranger 7 was built to photograph the Moon as it approached, then intentionally impact the surface. It returned 4,316 images, including increasingly detailed views that could help scientists assess the lunar terrain.

The planned ending

The impact was not an accidental crash: it was part of the mission design. The final image was transmitted only about 2.3 seconds before impact. Ranger 7 was the first unequivocal success in the U.S. lunar exploration effort after 13 consecutive Ranger mission failures.

Its legacy

Its close-up images helped scientists understand that even apparently smooth lunar plains were covered with impact craters, and supported the conclusion that crewed landings in lunar mare regions were feasible. [3]

Question to explore: If a mission is designed to end on impact, should we call it a failure—or judge it by what it was built to discover?

NASA source: [3] Ranger 7 mission page

Moon · 1964 Robotic impact probe

Ranger 7

A mission with no planned landing—and a final sequence of images that brought the Moon into sharper focus than ever before.

Mission fact: Launched July 28, 1964 · Returned 4,316 images before lunar impact
Open the story

01 · A mission with a different destination

Ranger 7 did not carry astronauts, and it was not designed to land softly. Its mission was to approach the Moon, photograph the surface in increasing detail, and deliberately impact the lunar terrain.

Why plan a one-way journey? Scientists needed close-up photographs to study the lunar surface and help identify areas that might be suitable for future crewed landings. Ranger 7 was a camera-carrying reconnaissance spacecraft: its images were the mission's most important scientific return.

02 · The countdown begins

Ranger 7 launched on July 28, 1964, aboard an Atlas-Agena B launch vehicle. It travelled toward the Moon carrying six television cameras designed to photograph the surface during its final approach.

As the spacecraft drew closer, its cameras captured views at progressively finer scales. The goal was not simply to take a beautiful picture of the Moon, but to reveal terrain features too small to study adequately from Earth.

03 · Images from the final seconds

On July 31, 1964, Ranger 7 reached the Moon. Before impact, it transmitted 4,316 images according to NASA's mission overview. Its final image arrived about 2.3 seconds before the spacecraft struck the surface.

Imagine the scientific value of those last pictures: details of a distant landscape arriving on Earth moments before the camera-carrying spacecraft reached its destination. The impact was intentional, not an unexpected accident.

04 · What the photographs revealed

The close-up photographs revealed a heavily cratered lunar surface, including details that could not be resolved clearly in earlier Earth-based observations. They strengthened scientists' understanding of the terrain and helped assess the feasibility of landing spacecraft in the lunar maria.

Ranger 7 was the first fully successful mission in the Ranger series. Earlier missions had experienced a mixture of failures and partial successes, making this achievement an important milestone in American lunar exploration.

Mission record

  • Launch: July 28, 1964.
  • Lunar impact: July 31, 1964.
  • Destination: The Moon, near Mare Cognitum.
  • Imaging equipment: Six television cameras.
  • Images returned: 4,316, according to NASA's mission overview.
  • Surface operating time: None; the spacecraft was designed to impact the Moon.
  • What remains: The impact remnants remain at the lunar impact site.

Mars · 2018–2022Lander

InSight

It listened to Mars’s tremors until its dwindling power could no longer keep the mission alive.

Mission fact: Launched May 5, 2018; landed November 26, 2018, at Elysium Planitia.
Open the story

The mission

InSight studied Mars’s interior. Its SEIS instrument measured seismic activity, while HP³ was designed to investigate heat flow beneath the surface. The flat landing region at Elysium Planitia was selected in part to provide a safe place to land and deploy the instruments. Two tiny Mars Cube One (MarCO) spacecraft also flew as communications technology demonstrators on the journey to Mars.

Why it stopped

Dust gradually accumulated on InSight’s solar panels, reducing the electricity available to run the lander. As power declined, the mission could no longer maintain operations; NASA declared the mission over in December 2022 after repeated attempts to contact it received no response. The dust-related power loss is documented; we should not claim a more specific cause for every final event unless a source establishes it.

Its legacy

InSight recorded marsquakes and provided measurements that improved scientists’ understanding of Mars’s crust, mantle, and core. The lander remains on Mars, a reminder that even a mission that stops communicating can leave valuable scientific data behind.

Question to explore: How can scientists keep learning from data sent home by a machine that can no longer answer?

Mars · 1997Lander + rover

Mars Pathfinder & Sojourner

A small rover investigated a world of rocks, magnetic dust, and clues to a wetter past.

Mission fact: Landed July 4, 1997; the mission’s final data transmission was September 27, 1997.
Open the story

The mission

Pathfinder demonstrated an airbag-assisted landing system and delivered Sojourner, the first rover to operate on Mars. The lander and rover returned thousands of images and measurements of rocks, soil, and weather.

The ending

NASA records the final Pathfinder data transmission on September 27, 1997. The mission ended after the lander’s power system failed, removing the communications relay Sojourner depended on. The rover and lander were left at the landing site; the mission did not return them to Earth.

Its legacy

Rounded pebbles and cobbles suggested water-formed conglomerates in a warmer past. Scientists also studied magnetic airborne dust, observed dust devils, and found evidence consistent with Mars having once been warmer and wetter. Pathfinder demonstrated landing and rover technologies that influenced later Mars exploration. [10][11]

Question to explore: What could a handful of pebbles and a thin layer of magnetic dust reveal about a planet’s ancient environment?

NASA sources: [10] Mars Pathfinder mission page [11] NASA JPL: Pathfinder / Sojourner

Mars · 2021–2024Technology demonstrator

Ingenuity Mars Helicopter

Designed for a handful of test flights, Ingenuity flew 72 times—and helped open a new way to explore another planet.

Mission fact: First flight April 19, 2021; final flight January 18, 2024.
Open the story

The mission

Ingenuity was a technology demonstration intended to prove that powered, controlled flight was possible in Mars’s thin atmosphere. It was originally planned for up to five test flights over about 30 days.

Beyond the plan

Ingenuity completed 72 flights over almost three years. During its 72nd flight on January 18, 2024, it lost contact with Perseverance during descent. Later images showed rotor-blade damage from landing, leaving it unable to fly. NASA’s account says the exact cause of the communications dropout and the helicopter’s orientation at touchdown were still being investigated at the time of the mission-end announcement.

Its legacy

Ingenuity proved that powered, controlled flight could work on another planet and demonstrated how aerial scouting could help plan rover routes and assess terrain. Its flight data will inform future ideas for aerial exploration. The helicopter remains at its final airfield on Mars. [12][13][14]

Question to explore: If a tiny aircraft can scout another world, what might the next generation of planetary explorers be able to do?

NASA sources: [12] Ingenuity mission page [13] NASA mission-end announcement [14] Ingenuity at its final airfield

Beyond the mission

Hardware stays. Knowledge travels.

Not every abandoned machine tells the same story. Ranger 7 was designed to impact the Moon. Surveyor 3 became a place astronauts could visit. InSight lost the power needed to keep working. Ingenuity exceeded its flight plan by a remarkable margin. Their endings differ—but each left evidence, engineering lessons, or new questions for future explorers.

The central idea: “abandoned” does not mean “meaningless.” A mission’s legacy includes what it discovered, what engineers learned, and what future explorers can still study.
